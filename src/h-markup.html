Vancouver federal & provincial poll atlas

2025 federal polling divisions with provincial voting areas overlaid, for comparing federal and provincial voting poll by poll in the City of Vancouver.

Provincial boundaries are not bundled here because Elections BC publishes them under their own licence. Open the Data tab and load a voting-area file to draw the overlay — a zipped shapefile, KML, KMZ or GeoJSON all work, in BC Albers or lon/lat.

1. Provincial voting-area boundaries

Get these from Elections BC's GIS spatial data page or the BC Data Catalogue, and load the voting-area file for the election you are comparing against. A zipped shapefile (.shp + .dbf + .prj), .kml, .kmz or .geojson all work. BC Albers, UTM zone 10N and lon/lat are converted automatically. Nothing is uploaded anywhere — the file is read in this browser tab.

2. Federal poll-by-poll results

Elections Canada publishes one pollresults_resultatsbureau<riding>.csv per electoral district. Load them one at a time or as a .zip; each file adds to what is already loaded.

3. Provincial results by voting area

Elections BC publishes results by voting area for each electoral district. Long files (one row per candidate) and wide files (one column per party) are both read.

Build the crosswalk

Federal polling divisions and provincial voting areas are drawn by different agencies and share no boundaries, so results cannot be joined on a key. This measures how much of each federal division falls inside each voting area and splits votes in those proportions. Read the Method tab before quoting any number from here.

How the two geographies are reconciled

Federal polling divisions and provincial voting areas are drawn independently and share almost no boundaries. There is no key to join them on, so this atlas estimates the overlap directly: it lays a lattice of equally spaced points over the area, records which federal division and which voting area each point falls in, and treats the counts as areas. The share of a federal division lying inside a given voting area becomes the fraction of that division's votes assigned to it.

What that assumes

  • Votes are spread evenly within a poll. This is the standard areal-interpolation assumption and the main caveat on every number here. Where a poll mixes a tower block with a park, the even-spreading assumption is wrong, and no amount of extra sampling resolution fixes it.
  • Area is a stand-in for voters. Weighting by population would be better. If you can get dissemination-block counts, weighting the lattice by block population would be the natural next step.
  • Small overlaps are discarded. Two agencies digitise the same street slightly differently, so nearly every boundary pair produces hairline slivers that hold no voters. Overlaps below the chosen threshold are dropped and the remaining shares are rescaled to carry exactly the vote mass the slivers held.

Polls with no real boundary

Elections Canada marks mobile polls (type M, numbered from 500) and single-building polls (type S, numbered from 400). Mobile polls serve care homes and similar institutions; their polygons are token markers a few metres across, not catchments. They are excluded from the map by default. Because the lattice can miss polygons that small, any polygon the lattice barely sees is instead assigned whole to the unit containing an interior point of it, so its votes are never silently lost.

The coverage problem you cannot sample your way out of

Advance polls, special ballots and mail-in votes have no boundary in either agency's geography. They are a large and growing share of the vote, and they are not distributed like election-day votes. Whenever results are loaded, the Data tab reports what fraction of the votes in the file landed in a mapped poll. Treat any correlation here as a statement about election-day ordinary votes, and quote that coverage figure alongside it.

Reading the correlation

Both the unweighted and the vote-weighted Pearson coefficient are shown. They differ because small polls are numerous and noisy; the weighted figure is usually the more honest summary of how the electorate actually behaved, while the unweighted one describes how the polls behave as units. Spearman's rank coefficient is given as a check against a handful of outliers driving the fit. Note that units are not independent observations — neighbouring polls resemble each other — so the confidence interval is narrower than the spatial autocorrelation really warrants.

Choosing the common geography

Redistributing onto provincial voting areas moves federal votes and leaves provincial votes untouched; onto federal divisions it does the reverse. Overlap pieces move both and give the most units, but each is smaller and noisier. If a result only holds on one of the three, it is a property of the method rather than of the electorate.
